Fleet profiles

Angel-class stratospheric bio-glider

Out on hydrogen, home on sunlight

A hybrid flight profile reaches any point on Earth in under two hours with zero emissions.

  1. Mag-drop. Released from Midway at 30 km, the glider free-falls along the tether until inductors bend it into a J-curve.
  2. Ignition. Levelling out at 25–27 km and Mach 3, its hydrogen fuel-cell scramjets light and push it to Mach 6+. The only exhaust is water vapor.
  3. Release. Over the target it descends and opens barometric canisters 1,000 m above the burn scar.
  4. Return. Solar wings trickle-charge electric turbines on the glide home; it docks at Midway or lands on a mega-city runway.
Leviathan-class pelagic harvester · “The Whales”

The oceanic equivalent of the Angels

Wide-stance trimarans twice the size of the largest aircraft carriers, flying their own aircraft off a full flight deck. Aura runs the labor and navigation; the upper decks are a small city built for people.

Drone hives
Internal drydocks launch, recharge and offload data from thousands of reef-restoration submersibles and nutrient-plume injectors.
Recycling forges
Kilometer-wide electromagnetic nets take in macro-plastics, ghost nets and chemical pollutants; onboard foundries print drone parts and coral-seed scaffolds.
Research campus
Sealed upper-deck habitats, oceanographic labs and deep-sea observation lounges, open to teams under the Accord.
Abyss mission
Crews live aboard for months and dive manned submersibles through moon pools to trenches and hydrothermal vents.

How an Angel takes off

Primary · secondary

Angels leave Earth Port two ways. Normally they hang beneath the Stratosphere Port and drop into a magnetic slingshot that never touches the thick air below 20 km. When weather or tether maintenance rules that out, a mag-lev catapult throws them off a titanium runway at sea level.

The vertical maglev

The tether is the launch track

  1. Drop. The glider falls parallel to the tether on a frictionless magnetic cushion while gravity builds raw speed.
  2. Bend. The tether's field acts as a magnetic half-pipe: a wall of force that turns the dive into a horizontal sweep. Flaps would find nothing to push against in air this thin.
  3. Push-out. At the bottom of the curve, the tether gives the glider one last electromagnetic shove outward.

The hardest part of the flight runs on the elevator's power grid, so an Angel never carries the thousands of pounds of rocket fuel it would need to reach speed on its own.

Primary method

The stratospheric mag-drop

  1. Suspension. The glider hangs from an electromagnetic rail mount in the Stratosphere Port's lower housing; hydraulic clamps back up the magnetic harness during checks.
  2. Conditioning. Below 12 hPa, the bio-luminescent skin and structural membranes are pressurized and thermally preconditioned.
  3. Systems check. Avionics confirm mag-rail polarity and clear corridors in the upper mesosphere and lower stratosphere.
  4. Free-fall. Released, it drops parallel to the tether, building speed in the thin air.
  5. Deflection. Linear inductors on the tether push on its ventral bio-metallic frame, bending the fall outward into a J-curve; living wing spars stiffen against up to 9.5 G.
  6. Ignition. At the base of the curve, 25–27 km up and Mach 3, the scramjets light and carry it to a Mach 6 cruise.
Secondary method

The Earth Port runway catapult

  1. Runway. A 3.5 km titanium composite strip on the floating terminal at the tether's base, treated against salt and exhaust heat.
  2. Catapult. A mag-lev sled forces the heavy glider through the dense sea-level air.
  3. Ejection. The glider leaves the sled at the end of the track at Mach 1.5 or more.
  4. Climb. It pitches up hard, punches through the troposphere and levels off in its stratospheric cruise corridor.

The Apex Station

36,000 km up

At geostationary orbit the tether ends in a colossal station whose mass keeps the whole ribbon under tension. It is the counterweight, a mine, a lab and humanity's main off-world shipyard.

Asteroid core
A captured near-Earth asteroid at the centre is the anchor mass and a zero-gravity mine for rare-earth metals and silicates.
Orbital labs
Pressurized long-term habitats for researchers, astrophysicists and engineers working under the Accord.
Parasol forges
New thin-film panels for the L1 sunshade, made from asteroid material and deployed with no atmospheric launch.
Interstellar drydock
Scaffolding on the outer ring for a generation ship, built entirely in orbit, free of Earth's gravity well.
Kessler remediation fleet

Keeping the high road open

A swarm of autonomous sweepers launches from the station to capture dead satellites, spent rocket stages and micro-shrapnel, then melts them down or de-orbits them. That stops a cascading chain of collisions, the Kessler syndrome, before it can start.
